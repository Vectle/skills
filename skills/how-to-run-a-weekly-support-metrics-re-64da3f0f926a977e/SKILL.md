## TL;DR
A weekly metrics review is 30 minutes, five numbers, and one action per red metric. Volume trend, CSAT, first-contact resolution, backlog age, and escalations. Anything more and the meeting becomes a report nobody reads. The whole point is the action list: every red number leaves the room with an owner and a date.

## The query

```text
how to run a weekly support metrics review
```

## Use this when

- Metrics exist but nobody acts on them
- Starting a metrics habit from zero
- The team is surprised by the same problems monthly
- Leadership wants a regular support update

## Not for

- Quarterly business reviews
- Incident postmortems
- Deep-dive analytics sessions
- Real-time operational standups

## Steps

### 1. Fix the five numbers and the owner

Volume trend, CSAT, first-contact resolution, oldest backlog ticket age, and escalation count. One person owns pulling them before the meeting, same format every week. If the numbers are assembled live in the meeting, you have already lost.

Expected output: five numbers in a fixed template, ready before the meeting starts.

### 2. Timebox to 30 minutes, same slot weekly

Ten minutes on the numbers, twenty on the reds. Greens get a nod, not a discussion. The timebox is what keeps this a ritual instead of a chore people dodge.

Expected output: a short recurring meeting people actually attend.

### 3. Color the numbers, then discuss only red and yellow

Green means within target, yellow means drifting, red means missed. Set the thresholds once and stop debating them weekly. Discussion time goes to red first, yellow if time remains.

Expected output: a shared, stable definition of good versus bad for each metric.

### 4. One action per red, with an owner and a date

Not "look into CSAT". Instead: "Priya pulls 20 low-CSAT tickets by Wednesday and posts the top theme." Vague actions die. Named owners with dates get done or get visibly missed, which is also useful.

Expected output: an action list where every item has a name and a deadline.

### 5. Open next week with last week's actions

First five minutes: did the actions happen, and did they move the number. Unfinished actions roll forward exactly once, then escalate. This is what turns the review from theater into a machine.

Expected output: a visible record of actions completed and numbers moved.

## Ready-to-use review template

```text
WEEK OF: [date]        OWNER: [name]

VOLUME:      [n] tickets ([up/down] x% vs last week)   [green/yellow/red]
CSAT:        [n]%  (responses: [n])                    [green/yellow/red]
FCR:         [n]%  (reopen window: 7 days)             [green/yellow/red]
OLDEST OPEN: [n] days                                 [green/yellow/red]
ESCALATIONS: [n]  ([n] still open)                    [green/yellow/red]

LAST WEEK'S ACTIONS:
- [action] | [owner] | done / rolled / escalated

THIS WEEK'S ACTIONS:
- [action] | [owner] | due [date]
```

## Variant phrasings

### weekly customer support KPI review

Same five steps. KPI is just the corporate word for the five numbers.

### support metrics meeting agenda

The template above is the agenda. Ten minutes numbers, twenty minutes reds, five minutes actions.

### how to track support team performance weekly

Steps 1, 3, and 5. Tracking is the template plus the follow-through; the meeting is just the container.

## Why it happens

Support metrics usually live in a dashboard nobody opens, so problems get discovered monthly when they are already expensive. The weekly review works because it is small enough to sustain and action-oriented enough to matter. Teams do not fail at metrics from lack of data; they fail from lack of a forcing function that converts a red number into a named human doing a thing by a date.

## Edge cases

- Everything is red: pick the two that hurt most and ignore the rest for a month. Five simultaneous fires means no fires get put out.
- Everything is green for months: raise a threshold or swap in a harder metric. Comfortable greens mean the review is coasting.
- The owner role rotates and quality drops: keep one permanent backup owner. Rotation is nice; consistency is necessary.
- Remote async teams: run it as a written thread with a 24-hour action deadline instead of a meeting. Same template, same rules.
- Leadership crashes the meeting: give them the template afterward. An audience turns a working review into a performance.
