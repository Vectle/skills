## TL;DR
Benchmarking support means experiencing competitors the way customers do: file the same ticket with three competitors and with yourself, time everything, and score the same dimensions. Measure first response time, resolution quality, channels offered, and self-serve depth. Present the gaps that matter to buying decisions, not vanity stats. And benchmark to find your edge, not to copy theirs.

## The query

```text
how to benchmark support against competitors
```

## Use this when

- Leadership asks how you compare
- Repositioning support as a differentiator
- Before a pricing change
- Choosing where to invest next

## Not for

- Copying competitors feature for feature
- Scraping review sites for scores
- One-off anecdote wars in meetings
- Spying on private customer data

## Steps

### 1. Pick three competitors and one realistic ticket

Choose the competitors you actually lose deals to. Write one ticket a real customer would file: specific, mid-complexity, answerable. Use the same ticket everywhere so the comparison is apples to apples.

Expected output: three competitors and one standardized test ticket.

### 2. Mystery-shop every channel they offer

File the ticket through each competitor's chat, email, and help center. Time the first response, count the touches to resolution, and note whether the answer was actually correct. Screenshot everything; memory lies about other people's support.

Expected output: timed, documented runs for each competitor and channel.

### 3. Run the same ticket through your own team

Blind if you can: have someone outside support file it so your team does not know it is a test. Score yourself on the identical dimensions. Most teams discover they grade themselves on intentions and competitors on outcomes. The same ticket fixes that.

Expected output: your own scores, measured the same way, no home-team discount.

### 4. Score self-serve separately

Search each help center for the ticket's keywords before filing. A competitor with great docs and slow chat is a different animal than one with no docs and fast chat. Customers try self-serve first, so score it like it matters, because it does.

Expected output: a self-serve score per competitor: found, partial, or missing.

### 5. Present two or three gaps that matter

Nobody acts on a 40-row spreadsheet. Pick the gaps that affect buying decisions: response time on sales-adjacent tickets, resolution quality on complex issues, a channel you lack. Frame each as the customer experience, not the metric.

Expected output: a short, vivid comparison leadership remembers.

## Variant phrasings

### competitor support analysis

Steps 1 through 4, written up as a doc. Analysis is the method; benchmarking is the comparison.

### how does our customer support compare

The full five steps, ending with the presentation in step 5. The question is always really asking for the story, not the spreadsheet.

### customer service benchmarking best practices

Step 3 is the practice most teams skip. Benchmarking without self-scoring is just competitor admiration.

## Why it happens

Teams benchmark support because "are we good" is unanswerable in isolation. But most benchmarking is either review-site scores, which measure loud minorities, or sales anecdotes, which measure whatever the last lost deal complained about. Mystery shopping the same ticket is the only method that measures the actual customer experience on equal terms, and the self-scoring step is what keeps it honest.

## Edge cases

- Competitors with no public support: that is data. Score the absence; some buyers read it as a red flag and some as efficiency.
- You discover a competitor is clearly better: good. That is the most valuable outcome. Present it plainly with the ticket evidence.
- Legal worries about mystery shopping: you are using a public service as a customer would. Do not misrepresent your identity beyond a normal test account.
- Benchmarks go stale: re-run yearly or after a competitor announces a support overhaul. Old benchmarks become folklore.
- Leadership wants to copy the winner: push back. Their winner serves their customers and their economics. Steal the principle, not the playbook.
