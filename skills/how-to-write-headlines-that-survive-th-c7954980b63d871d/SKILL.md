## TL;DR

Headlines that survive the SERP put the keyword first, stay under 60 characters, and say specifically what the reader gets. Clever headlines die in search results because nobody searches for clever. Write the headline the searcher would type, then make it human.

```text
how to write headlines that survive the SERP
```

## Use this when

- Titling articles or skills meant to rank
- Rewriting headlines with impressions but no clicks
- Setting title tag conventions

## Not for this skill when

- The headline is for social (curiosity works there)
- The brand voice demands cleverness (know the trade-off)
- You are writing the H1 only and ignoring the title tag (fix both)

## Steps

1. Start with the query. What would the searcher type? Put those words at the front of the headline. Expected: the first 3-4 words match the query.

2. Keep it under 60 characters. Longer titles get truncated with an ellipsis, which kills the click. Expected: the full headline visible in results.

3. Be specific, not clever. Numbers, tool names, and outcomes beat wordplay. Expected: a stranger knows what they get before clicking.

```
headline check:
  keyword front: yes
  under 60 chars: yes
  specific promise: yes
  matches content: yes
```

4. Match the content exactly. The headline is a promise; the page must keep it in the first screen. Expected: no bounce from mismatch.

5. Test with impressions data. Headlines with impressions but no clicks are the rewrite list; change one variable at a time. Expected: CTR climbs on rewritten titles.

## Variant phrasings

### SEO headline writing

Query words first, under 60 characters, specific promise, content matches.

### titles that get clicked in search

Front-loaded keywords plus a concrete payoff, no truncation, no cleverness.

### headline formulas for ranking content

[Query] + [specific outcome], kept short enough to display fully.

## Why it happens

Searchers scan results in under two seconds, matching their query words and judging specificity. Google may rewrite your title, but a well-formed original survives most rewrites; a clever one gets replaced or truncated into meaninglessness.

## Edge cases / pitfalls

- Google rewrites titles it dislikes; a clear original minimizes this.
- Do not keyword-stuff; one natural query phrase is enough.
- The H1 can be slightly more human than the title tag; use both deliberately.
- Brand names at the end, never the front, unless the brand is the query.
